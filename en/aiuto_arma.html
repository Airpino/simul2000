Module help

Simulation of ARMA models

A time series is generated from an ARMA(p, q) model, possibly integrated, and we look at its path, its autocorrelations and the distribution of its values.

What it shows

The model is

Xₜ = φ₁Xₜ₋₁ + … + φₚXₜ₋ₚ + εₜ + θ₁εₜ₋₁ + … + θ_qεₜ₋q

with standard normal innovations εₜ. The module generates the series value by value and shows, in four windows, the latest values scrolling by, the whole series, the empirical ordinary and partial autocorrelations compared with the theoretical ones, and the histogram of the values. You learn to recognise a model from its autocorrelations.

How to use it

The settings screen

  • AR (φ) and MA (θ) coefficients, separated by “;”: p and q follow from their number, and an empty field means p = 0 or q = 0. If the AR part is not stationary the model is rejected with an explanation; if the MA part is not invertible only a warning appears.
  • Total length (1,000, 10,000 or 100,000 values) and window of the partial series, i.e. how many values to show in the first window.
  • Integration: none, for an ARMA(p, q), or once, for an ARIMA(p, 1, q).
  • Lags of the autocorrelations: how many to show, from 5 to 50.

During the simulation

  • Partial series: the latest values, scrolling to the left; at low speed the series is seen forming point by point.
  • Empirical autocorrelations: ordinary and partial, as bars, with the theoretical ones as ticks and the ±2/√n bands; the table tab gives them as numbers, with mean and variance.
  • Whole series, with the stretch of the partial series highlighted, and histogram of the values with the theoretical normal N(0, γ₀).
  • The speed ranges from 5 to 20,000 values per second; the windows can be resized and maximised.

Teaching suggestions

AR(1): persistence and alternation

Compare φ = 0.9 and φ = −0.9 with θ empty: in the first case the series has long slow swings and the autocorrelations decrease as 0.9ʰ; in the second the series changes sign at every step and the autocorrelations alternate in sign.

Telling AR from MA

In an MA(1) the ordinary autocorrelations vanish after lag 1, while the partial ones decay; in an AR(2) the opposite happens: the partial ones vanish after lag 2. These can serve as empirical guidelines for identifying the model from an actually observed series.

The random walk

With φ and θ empty and integration once you get a random walk: the series wanders without returning to a level, the ordinary autocorrelations are all close to 1 and only the partial one at lag 1 is large. There is no longer a theoretical distribution of the values.

Stationarity

Try φ = 1.1: the model is rejected because the series would explode. With φ₁ = 0.5 and φ₂ = 0.6 the rejection is less obvious: the stationarity conditions concern the coefficients jointly, not one at a time.

The ±2/√n bands

Even for a model whose theoretical autocorrelations are zero beyond a certain lag, about one empirical autocorrelation in twenty falls outside the bands: the significance level at work, and an invitation not to over-interpret correlograms.

Technical details

Stationarity and invertibility are checked by turning the coefficients into partial autocorrelations with the backward Durbin-Levinson recursion: the model is stationary if they are all less than 1 in absolute value, that is, if the roots of the AR polynomial lie outside the unit circle. The theoretical autocovariances come from the ψ weights of the MA(∞) representation:

γ(h) = Σⱼ ψⱼ ψⱼ₊ₕ,    ψ₀ = 1,   ψⱼ = θⱼ + Σᵢ φᵢ ψⱼ₋ᵢ

and the partial autocorrelations from the Durbin-Levinson recursion. 500 burn-in values are discarded before the series is recorded. The empirical autocovariances are updated at each new value with cumulative sums, so the computation stays fast even with 100,000 values.
