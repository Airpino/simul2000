Bayesian inference

A prior distribution is updated with the data, one observation at a time. You see how prior and likelihood combine in the posterior, how much the prior matters as n grows, and how credible intervals behave when the experiment is repeated many times.

What it shows

The module uses the three most common conjugate models, in which the posterior has the same form as the prior:

  • Proportion: 0/1 data with probability θ, beta prior.
  • Mean of a normal with known σ: normal prior.
  • Rate of a Poisson: counts with mean θ, gamma prior.

In all three the prior is chosen with two numbers with a simple meaning: the prior mean m₀ and the weight n₀, that is how many observations the prior is “worth”. Then the posterior mean is always

E(θ | data) = (n₀ · m₀ + n · x̄) / (n₀ + n)

a weighted average between what was believed before and what the data say. The large chart shows the prior, the (normalised) likelihood and the posterior with its credible interval; the one below, the path of the posterior mean and of the maximum likelihood estimate as n grows. Once the experiment is over you can repeat it thousands of times to measure coverage, bias and mean squared error of the Bayesian and of the frequentist estimates.

How to use it

The choices screen

  • Model and true value of θ: θ is the parameter that generates the data; for the normal you also choose σ.
  • Prior distribution: m₀ and n₀. Three buttons suggest a vague prior, an informative prior centred on the true value, and an informative but wrong one. Below you read the corresponding hyperparameters, for example Beta(α, β) with α = m₀n₀ and β = (1 − m₀)n₀.
  • Data: total number of observations N, how often to update, level of the intervals and number of repetitions of the experiment.

During the simulation

  • “Start” adds the observations one at a time (or in groups); the table shows the current estimates and the weighted-average formula with the actual numbers.
  • The checkboxes on the right hide the likelihood or the prior when the chart is too crowded.
  • “Repeat the experiment” reruns the whole experiment, with N observations, thousands of times, and compares credible and confidence intervals, posterior mean and maximum likelihood.

Teaching suggestions

The prior counts as n₀ observations

With the proportion, θ = 0.3 and the “informative and wrong” prior (n₀ = 20), advance slowly: after 20 observations the weight of the data is 50%, after 180 it is 90%. The path of the posterior mean starts from m₀ and catches up with the maximum likelihood one.

A flat prior reproduces the frequentist results

With the normal mean and n₀ = 0 the posterior coincides with the normalised likelihood, and the credible interval with the confidence interval: the two curves on the chart overlap.

The price and the reward of an informative prior

Repeat the experiment with N = 20 and the right informative prior: the posterior mean has a much smaller mean squared error than maximum likelihood, and the intervals cover more than stated. With the wrong prior the opposite happens: strong bias and low coverage. The repetitions chart shows the distribution of the posterior mean shifted towards m₀.

Two readings of the same interval

The credible interval reads “θ lies here with probability 95%, given the prior and the data”; the confidence interval “the method captures θ 95% of the time”. With a vague prior the two intervals nearly coincide: a good moment to discuss the difference in meaning, not in numbers.

Technical details

With S the sum of the data and n their number:

Beta(m₀n₀, (1−m₀)n₀) → Beta(m₀n₀ + S, (1−m₀)n₀ + n − S)
N(m₀, σ²/n₀) → N((n₀m₀ + S)/(n₀ + n), σ²/(n₀ + n))
Gamma(m₀n₀, n₀) → Gamma(m₀n₀ + S, n₀ + n)

The gamma is parametrised by shape and rate. Credible intervals are equal-tailed, with the quantiles of the beta, normal and gamma computed from the incomplete beta and gamma functions. The likelihood is drawn normalised to integrate to 1, that is as the posterior one would get with a flat prior. The frequentist intervals used for comparison are the Wald ones: p̂ ± z√(p̂(1−p̂)/n), x̄ ± zσ/√n and x̄ ± z√(x̄/n).
