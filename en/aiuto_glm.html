Module help

Generalised linear models

The same four windows as the regression module, for models whose response is binary, a count or a positive quantity: logistic, Poisson and gamma regression.

What it shows

In generalised linear models the mean of the response, μᵢ = E(Yᵢ), is linked to the linear predictor ηᵢ = β₀ + β₁xᵢ by a link function: ηᵢ = g(μᵢ). The module draws many samples from the chosen exponential family, estimates β₀ and β₁ by maximum likelihood and shows their sampling distribution, comparing it with the asymptotic normal. The windows are the same as in regression: fitted curves, pairs of estimates, single sample and text.

How to use it

  • Distribution:
    • binomial, with logit link: with one trial per x the responses are 0 or 1, and this is logistic regression; with more trials per x, the vertical axis shows the proportion of successes;
    • Poisson, with log link: the responses are counts;
    • gamma, with log link (instead of the canonical reciprocal link) and shape parameter ν, which is estimated together with the β.
  • Linear predictor: β₀ and β₁ (suggested 0.2 and 1.1) and the x, with the same options as in regression; the suggested x are standardised.
  • Simulation parameters, controls and windows as in regression.

When in a sample the maximum likelihood estimate does not exist, for example with perfectly separated binary responses or with all-zero counts, the sample is counted separately and flagged in the title of the second window and in the text.

Teaching suggestions

The logistic curves

With the binomial and one trial per x, the first window shows the fitted logistic curves around the true one, and the third the 0 and 1 responses with the estimated probabilities. A concrete way to show that the model estimates a probability, not the response.

When the estimate does not exist

With the logistic and n = 10 about one sample in eight has no maximum likelihood estimate: all the 0s lie on one side of the x and all the 1s on the other, and the most likely curve is a step. Increasing n or the number of trials per x makes the phenomenon disappear.

Asymptotic does not mean exact

With small n the simulated variances of the estimates exceed the asymptotic ones, and the cloud of pairs of estimates is not elliptical. Repeat with n = 10, 30, 100 and compare in the distributions of the b tab.

More trials per x

With 10 or 20 trials for each x the binomial approaches a normal, the observed proportions are close to the true curve and the estimates become much more precise: a link to the design of dose-response experiments.

The gamma and its shape parameter

With the gamma the text also reports the estimate of ν: with small n it is biased upwards, that is it underestimates the variability. Compare ν = 2 with ν = 20 to see how the scatter around the curve changes.

Technical details

The estimates are maximum likelihood, computed by Fisher scoring, i.e. iteratively reweighted least squares. Ellipses and curves use the asymptotic normal distribution of the estimates, with covariance matrix evaluated at the true parameter values:

Var(b) ≈ φ (X′WX)⁻¹

with W = diag(nᵢ pᵢ(1 − pᵢ)) for the binomial, diag(μᵢ) for the Poisson, the identity for the gamma with log link, and φ = 1/ν for the gamma, 1 otherwise. For the gamma the link is logarithmic instead of the canonical (reciprocal) one, which would need a positive predictor for every x. The shape parameter is estimated by solving with Newton’s method

log ν − ψ(ν) = d̄,    d̄ = (1/n) Σ [ −log(yᵢ/μ̂ᵢ) + (yᵢ − μ̂ᵢ)/μ̂ᵢ ]

where ψ is the digamma function; its asymptotic variance is 1/(n(ψ′(ν) − 1/ν)). An estimate is considered non-existent when the iterations diverge.
