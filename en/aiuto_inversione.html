Generation methods: inversion

A uniform number U is generated and transformed by the inverse of the distribution function: X = F⁻¹(U) has exactly the distribution F.

What it shows

The inversion method is the foundation of random number generation: if U is uniform on (0, 1) and F is a distribution function, then X = F⁻¹(U) has distribution F. The module shows it with an animation: for each number generated, a line starts from the value U on the vertical axis, reaches the graph of F and drops to the value X on the horizontal axis.

As numbers accumulate, the values U spread uniformly along the vertical axis, while the values X crowd where F rises fastest, that is where the density is highest.

How to use it

  • Distribution: the standard normal, or a discrete distribution with values 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and editable probabilities (suggested: 0.1; 0.4; 0.25; 0.1; 0.15). The probabilities must add up to 1.
  • Generate (or the Enter key) draws one number, with the animation of the lines; Clear starts from scratch.
  • Continuous generation: draws numbers continuously, from 1 to 50 per second.
  • Histograms of the generated values: on the left that of the U, with the uniform density; below that of the X, with the normal density or with the theoretical probabilities (the ticks on the bars). Best switched on after a number of draws.
  • Generated values: the last U and the corresponding X, and the list of all values.

Without histograms, the values collect along the axes: ticks for the U and for continuous X, counts under each value for discrete X.

Teaching suggestions

The discrete case: stretches as long as the probabilities

With the discrete distribution the distribution function is a step function, and each value k corresponds to a stretch of the vertical axis as long as its probability (the coloured bands). It is immediately clear why the value 2, with probability 0.4, comes up more often than the others.

Changing the probabilities

Change the probabilities, for example making one of them very small, and see how the steps and the frequencies change. A good exercise is to ask students to predict the frequencies before generating.

Uniform in, normal out

With the normal and the histograms on, after a few hundred numbers the histogram of the U is flat and that of the X is bell-shaped: the same uniformity, transformed by F⁻¹, produces the normal.

The tails

Values of U close to 0 or 1 produce values of X far from the centre: F is almost flat in the tails, and a small interval of U corresponds to a large interval of X. This is why extreme values are rare but possible.

Technical details

For the standard normal F⁻¹ has no closed form: Acklam’s algorithm is used, with an error |F(F⁻¹(u)) − u| below 10⁻⁷. For the discrete distribution X is the smallest value k for which F(k) ≥ U:

X = min { k : F(k) ≥ U }

The numbers U are produced by the base generator described in the general help; changing the seed or the distribution restarts the sequence.
