Module help

Monte Carlo integration

An integral is estimated by throwing random points into the unit square: the fraction of points falling below the curve approximates the area (but a better approximation is obtained by taking the mean of the values of f(x) computed at the generated points).

What it shows

The hit or miss method is the most intuitive of the Monte Carlo methods: uniform points are thrown into the unit square and the fraction of those falling below the graph of the function is counted. Since the area of the square is 1, that fraction estimates the integral of the function between 0 and 1. The module shows the points accumulating, the estimate converging to the true value and the confidence interval narrowing.

On request, the estimate with the sample-mean method, which uses only the abscissas, is computed on the same points: for the same number of points it is more precise.

How to use it

The settings screen

  • Function to integrate: parabola x² (integral 1/3), quarter circle √(1 − x²) (integral π/4), power x¹⁰ (integral 1/11), negative exponential e−x (integral 1 − 1/e). All lie within the unit square.
  • Total points: from 1,000 to 1,000,000; Update every: how often to redraw estimate and charts.
  • Confidence level: 90%, 95% or 99%.
  • Compare with the sample-mean method: adds the second estimate, computed on the same abscissas.
  • Colours: two colours for the points below and above the curve, red and green as in the original, or blue and orange.

During the simulation

  • The square fills up with points; when they are many, they become small squares so as not to cover each other.
  • Estimate of the integral: true value, estimate and confidence interval; below, the chart of the estimate as the number of points grows, on a log scale, with the band of the interval and the line of the true value.
  • Latest updates: a table with the latest estimates, their intervals and their width.

Teaching suggestions

Estimating π

With the quarter circle the integral is π/4: multiplying estimate and interval by 4 gives an estimate of π. It is a classic example that always arouses curiosity, and shows how slowly precision improves.

Precision grows as √n

Follow the width of the interval in the table of the latest updates: to divide it by 10 you need 100 times as many points. The log-scale chart makes this law visible.

When hit or miss is inefficient

With the power x¹⁰ the area below the curve is small, almost all the points fall above it, and the relative error is large. Compare the relative width of the interval with that of the parabola, for the same number of points.

Two methods on the same points

With the comparison on, two bands appear, the one of the sample-mean method narrower. At the bottom of the estimate panel you can read how many times more points hit or miss needs for the same precision: for the quarter circle about 3.4 times.

An integral we know

All the functions have a known integral: this is how the method can be checked. One can then discuss why, in several dimensions, Monte Carlo methods become preferable to deterministic numerical methods.

Technical details

With n points (Uᵢ, Vᵢ) uniform in the square, the hit or miss estimate is the fraction p̂ of points with Vᵢ ≤ f(Uᵢ); it is a binomial proportion with mean I, and the interval uses the normal approximation:

p̂ ± z · √(p̂ (1 − p̂) / n)

The sample-mean method estimates I by the mean of f(Uᵢ), with an interval based on the sample variance of f(Uᵢ). For a single trial the variances are

hit or miss: I (1 − I);    sample-mean method: ∫₀¹ f(x)² dx − I²

and their ratio tells how many times more points hit or miss needs to reach the same precision.
