Nonparametric inference

Three methods that do not need the shape of the distribution, tried out on normal and non-normal populations: the bootstrap, the permutation test and the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test.

What it shows

  • Bootstrap. A single sample is observed and thousands of bootstrap samples are drawn from it with replacement. Their distribution of the chosen statistic (mean, median, standard deviation, trimmed mean) builds up next to the true sampling distribution, which the simulator knows because it can draw many samples from the population. Estimated standard error and bias are compared with the true ones, and three bootstrap intervals are shown: percentile, “basic” and normal.
  • Permutation test. Two samples, the second possibly shifted by δσ. The group labels are reshuffled at random thousands of times: the distribution of the differences obtained is the one expected if the groups do not differ, and the fraction of permutations at least as extreme as the data is the p-value. Alongside, the p-values of the t test and of the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test on the same data.
  • Power: t and Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney. The two-sample experiment is repeated many times and the rejections of the two tests are counted. With δ = 0 you measure the type I error; with δ ≠ 0 the power, which accumulates on a chart as a function of δ together with the theoretical power of the t test for normal data.

There are seven populations: normal, uniform, exponential, lognormal, Laplace, Student’s t with 3 degrees of freedom and Cauchy, all rescaled to location μ and scale σ.

How to use it

  • On the choices screen you pick the experiment, the population and the experiment’s own settings: n and statistic for the bootstrap; n₁, n₂ and δ for two samples, with the number of permutations or repetitions.
  • In the bootstrap the strip at the top shows the observed sample and, below, the latest bootstrap sample: values drawn several times are stacked, values never drawn are missing. In the permutation test the strip shows how the latest permutation reshuffled the groups.
  • “New observed sample” draws new data from the same population and starts again, without going back to the choices.
  • In the power experiment the points stay on the chart until you change the shape, n₁, n₂ or α: change only δ and repeat.

Teaching suggestions

The bootstrap is centred on the estimate, not on the true value

With the mean of a normal and n = 20, press “New observed sample” several times: the bootstrap distribution moves with the estimate θ̂, but its width stays close to that of the true sampling distribution. It is this width, the standard error, that the bootstrap estimates well.

When the bootstrap struggles

The median with n = 10 gives a step-like bootstrap distribution, because it can only take the values in the sample. For the standard deviation of a lognormal with small n the bootstrap standard error often underestimates the true one.

Permutation and t say almost the same thing

With normal data the permutation p-value of the difference of means is very close to that of the t test: the t test is an excellent approximation of the permutation test. With the Cauchy the two part ways.

The price of ranks is small

With the normal, n₁ = n₂ = 12 and δ = 0.8, the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test loses only a few points of power to the t test. With the t with 3 degrees of freedom it gains about ten, with the lognormal more than twenty, with the Laplace a few; with the Cauchy the t test rejects about 5% of the time, Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney three times as often. With δ = 0 you check that both respect the level α.

Technical details

Bootstrap: with θ*1, …, θ*B the statistics of the bootstrap samples and qa their quantiles,

percentile: [qα/2, q1−α/2]   ·   basic: [2θ̂ − q1−α/2, 2θ̂ − qα/2]   ·   normal: θ̂ ± z · sd(θ*)

The true sampling distribution is estimated with up to 20,000 samples from the population, generated separately so the bootstrap sequence is unaffected. The population value of the trimmed mean is computed from 200,000 draws.

Permutation test: p-value = fraction of permutations with ∣d*∣ ≥ ∣dobs∣, with Monte Carlo standard error √(p(1−p)/P). Two-sample t test with pooled variance, n₁ + n₂ − 2 degrees of freedom. Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney: statistic U = R₁ − n₁(n₁+1)/2; for n₁, n₂ ≤ 25 the distribution of U is exact, computed by recursion, otherwise the normal approximation with continuity correction is used. All tests are two-sided.

The asymptotic relative efficiency of Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney relative to t, for shift alternatives, is 12σ²(∫f²)², computed numerically for the chosen shape: 3/π ≈ 0.955 for the normal, 1 for the uniform, 1.5 for the Laplace, 1.9 for the t with 3 degrees of freedom, infinite for the Cauchy.
